Which conductivity model should you trust for your next electrolyte?

It depends on what you already measured. On public CALiSol data for liquid Li-salt electrolytes, the model that wins on new formulations of familiar chemistry loses badly when asked for colder temperatures than it has seen, and fewer than a third of the source papers can be tested as genuinely new sources.

Three situations, three answers

New formulations in familiar chemistry

Your new mixture uses the same salt and solvents as existing data, at measured temperatures and concentrations.

Data requirement
Training data in the same chemistry systems.
Baselines scored
Extra Trees, Ridge, k-NN, gradient boosting, training median.
Strongest result
Extra Trees: 0.047 log10 (about 11%), lowest in all 7 source groups.
Evidence boundary
Groups come from 3 author groups; this is interpolation, not discovery.

Warm measurements → colder estimates

You measured a formulation at room temperature and above and need it below the coldest measurement.

Data requirement
At least three warm-side temperatures of the same formulation (for the Arrhenius fit).
Baselines scored
Per-formulation Arrhenius, Ridge (log target), Extra Trees.
Strongest result
Arrhenius 0.229 and Ridge 0.249 vs Extra Trees 0.536 log10; lower in every one of 6 groups.
Evidence boundary
Tested to 253 K and below. Near the glass transition or freezing, Arrhenius bends (VTF behaviour), so error grows with distance.

Transfer to a new source

You want a model trained on other papers to predict a lab or paper it has never seen.

Data requirement
Test conditions inside the training data's common support.
Baselines scored
Extra Trees, Ridge, k-NN, gradient boosting, training median.
Strongest result
Extra Trees: 0.075 log10 (about 19%) on 4 testable source groups.
Evidence boundary
Only 4 of 7 groups are testable; about half of the improvement seen after label cleaning comes from an easier test set.

Better labels, or an easier test?

Low conductivities read from linear-axis plots are unreliable. Removing them lowers error, but two different things happen: the test set gets easier, and the training labels get cleaner. Holding fold membership fixed separates the two. For cold extrapolation the whole improvement is an easier test set.

Is it the model or the temperature feature?

Refitting without 1/T or without T shows the cold-extrapolation result is mainly a model-family effect: Ridge with T alone (0.334) still beats Extra Trees with every feature (0.536). Trees predict a constant beyond the training range, whichever form of temperature they see.

What this does and does not show

Seven source groups drawn from three author groups are not independent laboratories; intervals resample groups and are conditional on that grouping. Results come from versions made after earlier test results had been seen, so they are evidence for further validation, not a leaderboard. Ten CALiSol rows with wrong temperatures were confirmed against the original paper and are excluded from the reference; the upstream report has not yet been submitted. No CALiSol measurement is shown on this page.
